Abstract

The “fewer moving parts” claim is true but incomplete. The EV drivetrain is simpler: torque is available from standstill, transmissions can be simpler, and far more of the energy used reaches the wheels. But the EV also needs a battery thermal management system working within a narrow temperature window, and its heavy battery affects tyre wear and, plausibly, chassis design. Maintenance falls, while cost risk shifts to the battery. Complexity has moved rather than vanished. Lighter, longer-lasting batteries could shrink that trade-off in future, but for now engineers have swapped one set of challenges for another.

1. Introduction

Just because Electric Vehicles (EVs) have fewer moving parts, doesn’t mean they are any less complex. EVs convert over 77% of grid electricity into power at the wheels, while ICE vehicles only convert around 16-25% of the energy in the fuel (U.S. Department of Energy, n.d.). Efficiency and simplicity are far from the same, an electric drivetrain is mechanically simpler than a combustion one. That said, the complexity moves into the battery, how it is cooled, and the extra weight the cars have to carry. So the question stands - if EVs are so much more efficient, are they purely the simpler machine? In my article, I will cover how EVs and ICE vehicles differ in their power source, efficiency, thermal management, weight, and maintenance.

2. Analysis

A major way engines lose energy is through friction. A modern combustion engine is most efficient around 1,000–2,500 rpm, so a transmission is required to adapt engine speed to wheel speed (Stadtfeld, 2020).. A modern car’s transmission typically has six to eight gears, which cause friction as they come into contact with one another. This, in turn, causes a large amount of energy to be lost to friction. Alternatively, EVs only need a single reduction gear because they can start from zero rpm and produce high torque at low speeds (Stadtfeld, 2020).. The drivetrain of an EV is much simpler than that of a combustion engine, meaning it loses far less energy through friction.

An even larger way an engine loses energy is through heat. In an internal combustion engine (ICE), fuel is burned to produce energy that turns the pistons, crankshaft, driveshaft, and eventually the wheels. However, having so many stages means that only 16–25% of the energy from the fuel reaches the wheels (J. Thomas, 2014, cited in US DOE, n.d.). When fuel is burned in the engine, a large portion of its energy is lost as heat, and the largest portion of heat loss occurs in the exhaust system (Murtdha S. Imran and Hasan T. Hashim, 2020). On the other hand, EVs don’t produce nearly as much heat via their electric motors, which allows them to use much more of the energy from the grid because less is lost as heat. This makes EVs far more efficient on paper.

The EV’s biggest hidden complication is its battery. Lithium-ion cells work best at about 25–40°C, with the temperature difference between cells kept below 5°C (Yavuz, Arıcı and Nižetić, 2023), although the exact range varies between studies. The stakes are high: above roughly 80°C, thermal runaway can be triggered, leading to fire or explosion (Liu et al., 2025). Most commercial EVs therefore use liquid-based thermal management, because liquids transfer heat well and can both cool and heat the pack (He, Saini and Chen, 2022). Under the same energy usage, liquid cooling can keep the maximum battery temperature about 3°C lower than air cooling (Liu et al., 2025). An EV may lack a traditional engine cooling system, but it needs a more precisely controlled one for its battery. This is the strongest evidence that fewer moving parts does not mean a simpler car.

Battery packs are heavy. Emissions Analytics, a specialist emissions-testing company, reports that testing suggests every extra 500 kg of vehicle weight, about the weight of a large battery, raises tyre wear emissions by 21% (Autobiz.ie, 2023). The company has been reported as saying there is no evidence that EVs emit more non-exhaust particles than other vehicles, because regenerative braking reduces wear (Fleet News, 2020). Weight is therefore a second place where complexity appears, and it plausibly affects chassis and suspension design too.

The battery also shifts where the cost risk lies. A Department of Energy study suggests EV batteries may last 12–15 years in moderate climates and 8–12 years in severe ones, and replacing them can be expensive (U.S. Department of Energy, n.d.). Day-to-day maintenance falls, but the financial risk moves to a single costly component.
