1. Introduction

Just because Electric Vehicles (EVs) have fewer moving parts, doesn’t mean they are any less complex. EVs convert over 77% of grid electricity into power at the wheels, while ICE vehicles only convert around 16-25% of the energy in the fuel (DOE, n.d). Efficiency and simplicity are far from the same, an electric drivetrain is mechanically simpler than a combustion one. That said, the complexity moves into the battery, how it is cooled, and the extra weight the cars have to carry. So the question stands - if EVs are so much more efficient, are they purely the simpler machine? In my article, I will cover how EVs and ICE vehicles differ in their power source, efficiency, thermal management, weight, and maintenance.

2. Analysis

2.1 Power Source and Transmission

A major way engines lose energy is through friction. A modern combustion engine is most efficient around 1,000–2,500 rpm, so a transmission is required to adapt engine speed to wheel speed (Stadtfeld, 2020).. A modern car’s transmission typically has six to eight gears, which cause friction as they come into contact with one another. This, in turn, causes a large amount of energy to be lost to friction. Alternatively, EVs only need a single reduction gear because they can start from zero rpm and produce high torque at low speeds (Stadtfeld, 2020).. The drivetrain of an EV is much simpler than that of a combustion engine, meaning it loses far less energy through friction.

2.2 Efficiency

An even larger way an engine loses energy is through heat. In an internal combustion engine (ICE), fuel is burned to produce energy that turns the pistons, crankshaft, driveshaft, and eventually the wheels. However, having so many stages means that only 16–25% of the energy from the fuel reaches the wheels (J. Thomas, 2014, cited in US DOE, n.d.). When fuel is burned in the engine, a large portion of its energy is lost as heat, and the largest portion of heat loss occurs in the exhaust system (Murtdha S. Imran and Hasan T. Hashim, 2020). On the other hand, EVs don’t produce nearly as much heat via their electric motors, which allows them to use much more of the energy from the grid because less is lost as heat.
